# Product requirements documents

A PRD aligns people on a problem, desired outcomes, and product requirements. It is not a giant feature description, implementation design, or backlog dump.

## Establish distinctions

- **Problem:** the user or business condition worth changing.
- **Requirement:** a condition the outcome must satisfy.
- **Solution:** a proposed experience or behavior that may satisfy requirements.
- **Implementation:** how the system will build the solution; link to technical design when substantial.

Do not disguise a chosen implementation as a user requirement.

## Adapt this structure

1. **Problem and evidence** — affected users, current behavior, impact, and evidence.
2. **Context** — strategic fit, prior attempts, related decisions.
3. **Goals and success criteria** — observable outcomes and measurement method.
4. **Non-goals** — explicit boundaries, not a parking lot of unrelated ideas.
5. **Users and use cases** — who, situation, need, and desired result.
6. **Requirements** — prioritized and testable functional and quality constraints.
7. **User experience** — critical flows, states, accessibility, and linked designs.
8. **Constraints and dependencies** — policy, platform, data, time, external teams.
9. **Risks and mitigations** — especially adoption, misuse, privacy, and operational risk.
10. **Rollout and learning** — release stages, safeguards, feedback, rollback.
11. **Open questions and decisions needed** — owner or next evidence when known.

Omit sections that do no work. Add status, owner, or target date only when the PRD's lifecycle needs them.

## Quality checks

- Can a reviewer trace each requirement to the problem or a constraint?
- Are success criteria measurable without prescribing implementation?
- Are assumptions and unknowns visible?
- Are edge states and excluded users or workflows explicit?
- Does the PRD link to authoritative research, policy, design, and technical decisions instead of copying them?
